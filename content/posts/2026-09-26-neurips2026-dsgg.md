## An Established Idea, a Different Lens

Scene graphs have been around for a while. Well before the current wave of multimodal foundation models, researchers were exploring how to represent a scene through its objects and their relationships. Work on [image retrieval using scene graphs dates back to 2015](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2015/html/Johnson_Image_Retrieval_Using_2015_CVPR_paper.html), and [Action Genome in 2020](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2020/papers/Ji_Action_Genome_Actions_As_Compositions_of_Spatio-Temporal_Scene_Graphs_CVPR_2020_paper.pdf) brought that structured view to actions and relationships unfolding in video. In AI years, the idea has had time to settle in.

A scene graph describes objects and their relationships: a person holding a cup, looking at a television, or standing beside a dog. A **dynamic** scene graph captures how those relationships change over time.

What drew us back was the opportunity to revisit this established idea with today's multimodal models, and to question some of the assumptions that had grown around the task. **What should a useful scene graph preserve, and are our benchmarks rewarding it?**

Much of DSGG evaluation has focused on recall: how many of the annotated relationships can the model recover? The catch is that a model can improve coverage by predicting more relations, while also adding incorrect or redundant ones. And even a correct relation may tell us very little about the main activity in the scene.

“Person standing on floor” and “floor beneath person” might both be true. If the person is looking into a closet, though, those two relations miss the interaction we would probably want to know about first.

[![Precision-recall curves and examples of redundant versus informative relations.](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/fig1.png)](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/fig1.png)

Putting on the **practical usefulness lens**, we revisit both how these graphs are evaluated and how they are generated. Alongside recall, we examine precision, the importance and diversity of ranked relations, and whether the resulting graphs help with video retrieval and question answering. We also introduce SGCls+, an evaluation setting that uses predicted boxes in place of ground-truth boxes.

## Reason First, Then Locate

Conventional DSGG pipelines typically detect objects first, then infer relationships from their regional features. Miss an object early, and that error can carry through the rest of the pipeline.

Our approach starts with a multimodal large language model reasoning over the video to predict interactions, before grounding the participating objects using SAM 3. We call this **reason-then-locate**.

[![Conventional locate-then-reason pipeline and our reason-then-locate framework.](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/fig5.png)](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/fig5.png)

Getting this to work takes more than asking a capable VLM to produce a graph. Two changes are particularly important: **how we represent interactions over time**, and **how we teach the model which relations deserve to come first**.

## Temporal Relation Sets: Less Repetition, Faster Generation

If someone holds a cup across several frames, a frame-wise representation asks the model to generate that same relation repeatedly. **Temporal Relation Sets (TRS)** represent the interaction once, with its start and end times:

[![Temporal relation tuple: subject, relation, object, start time and end time.](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/temporalrelationset.png)](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/temporalrelationset.png)

[![Frame-wise graphs repeat persistent interactions; TRS represents them with time intervals.](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/fig6.png)](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/fig6.png)

This changes the amount of work the model has to do. Shorter output sequences mean fewer tokens to train on and generate. In our comparison against the frame-wise MLLM baseline, TRS delivers:

| Dataset | Training speedup | Inference speedup |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Action Genome | **2.3×** | **3.4×** |
| VidVRD | **3.7×** | **8.6×** |

These are the measured reductions in training GPU hours and inference seconds per batch in our experiments, relative to the frame-wise representation.

[![TRS training and inference speedups alongside recall and precision ablations.](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/Fig8_Table9.png)](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/Fig8_Table9.png)

What I find encouraging is that this efficiency gain comes with better recall. For the Qwen3-VL backbone, **Recall@10 improves from 45.3 to 46.6 on Action Genome**, and **30.4 to 32.1 on VidVRD**. Precision@1 also improves by 0.8 points on both datasets in this ablation. Removing repetition gives us a more compact representation while preserving the interactions we want to recover.

## Importance-Aware Scoring: Learning What Should Come First

A compact graph still needs to prioritise useful information. We score relations using two criteria: **informativeness**, or how much a relation says about an important action or event in the video, and **diversity**, or how much it adds beyond the relations already selected.

For informativeness, an MLLM judges the relation against the video, using its normalised probability of answering “Yes” as the score. For diversity, we compare text embeddings of the relations: lower similarity to previously selected relations means less repetition. Their combined importance score determines the order of the training relations.

**Importance-Aware Finetuning (IAF)** then teaches the model to generate this ordered sequence. The training objective remains the standard next-token negative log-likelihood:

[![Negative log-likelihood: minus the sum of log probabilities of tokens given the video and preceding tokens.](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/ImportanceAware.png)](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/ImportanceAware.png)

The change is in the supervision: which relations appear first in the target sequence. To measure whether the model learns that ordering, we use **nDCG**, a ranking metric that rewards placing higher-importance relations nearer the top, relative to an ideal ordering.

[![Discounted cumulative gain normalised by the ideal ranking defines nDCG.](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/nDCG.png)](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/nDCG.png)

With IAF, the Qwen3-VL model's **nDCG@5 rises from 37.9 to 44.3**, and **nDCG@10 from 41.6 to 47.6**. InternVL3.5 shows the same pattern, improving from **35.7 to 43.0 at nDCG@5**. These comparisons isolate the effect of adding IAF to each backbone.

[![Table 5: ranking quality with and without IAF. Table 6: retrieval and question-answering results.](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/table5-6.png)](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/table5-6.png)

The retrieval curves make the benefit easier to see. When only a few relations can be used to retrieve a video, their order matters. Adding IAF improves retrieval with small sets of top-ranked relations. Including more relations does not always help: retrieval performance eventually falls even for our models, although it remains stronger than the earlier methods in this comparison.

[![Retrieval Recall at 5 versus number of top-ranked triplets, with and without IAF.](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/fig9.png)](/images/posts/neurips2026-dsgg/fig9.png)

## Does the Graph Help Us Understand the Video?

We evaluate the approach on **Action Genome, VidVRD and PVSG**, with improvements across these benchmarks. A few results from the Qwen3-VL-based model illustrate the difference:

- On Action Genome scene graph detection, **Recall@20 reaches 57.9**, compared with **45.2 for UNO**, under the with-constraint setting.
- On the same dataset's detection task, **Precision@10 reaches 48.4**, compared with **27.2 for OED**, using the paper's MLLM-as-a-Judge evaluation.
- On our scene graph question-answering benchmark, **accuracy reaches 48.9%, compared with 36.2% for OED**. Here, the answering model receives the predicted graph as context, rather than the video itself.

That last result is especially interesting to me. It asks whether the representation preserves enough of the scene to support reasoning beyond the individual relations. There is still room to improve: using the ground-truth graphs gives 63.8% on the same benchmark.

## What's Next

For me, this work connects back to the broader question of how we build AI systems that are grounded and operationally useful. If a scene graph is going to support downstream reasoning, we need to care about which information it preserves, how much noise it introduces, and whether it captures the interactions that matter.

Really grateful to this team for staying with the work through the rejections, and happy to finally see it heading to NeurIPS. Playing the long game in AI takes some patience. On this occasion, I'm very glad we did.
